How to watch animals

Four dogs, ten minutes. You cannot write down everything, so every field study picks a sampling rule. Jeanne Altmann's 1974 paper defined the rules primatologists still use, and today we run three of them on dogs.

Ad libitum: write down what you notice

No rule at all, the default of the notebook naturalist. Your eye goes to the loud wrestling match and skips the dog standing quietly, so conspicuous animals and dramatic behaviors are overrepresented: here the observer caught every play bout, one shake that happened near the action, and nothing else. Fine for generating ideas, useless for estimating rates. Altmann wrote her paper largely to get primatology off this method.

Focal-animal sampling: one animal, everything it does

Pick one animal in advance, record everything it does for a set window, then move to the next. Every individual gets equal effort, so time budgets and behavior rates come out unbiased, and the observer never misses what happened before an event because they were already watching. Team B runs 10-second focal windows: the fraction of windows where behavior changes is the baseline our shake data gets tested against. The window is 10 s because the shake window is 10 s, and two rates are only comparable over the same span of time.

All-occurrence sampling: every instance of one behavior

Watch the whole group and record every occurrence of a single behavior, wherever it happens. It works when the behavior is conspicuous enough to catch and rare enough to keep up with, which a shake is. It yields honest counts of the behavior and its context, but no denominator: you record the shakes that happened, never the shakes that didn't. Team A does this for shakes, which is why Team B's denominator matters.

Scan sampling: snapshots on a schedule

At fixed intervals, record each animal's state at that instant (the dots). Cheap, scales to large groups, and builds accurate time budgets. But a one-second event is almost never under a scan line: every shake in this figure falls between scans. Scan sampling cannot study shakes, which is why we don't use it today.

Altmann also described one-zero sampling (did the behavior occur at all in each interval, yes or no) mostly to warn against it: the score depends on the interval length you happened to choose and estimates neither rate nor duration.

The Shake-Off Study

A dog-park test of a published claim, using the observation methods of primate field studies.

The question

Do shakes mark transitions between behaviors more often than chance? Every dog owner has seen a dry dog do the full-body shake-off. Trainers and ethograms have labeled it a stress behavior for decades, yet only one study has ever measured what the behavior actually does in normal social life. The answer matters beyond dogs: animals from geese to macaques show short, stereotyped behaviors at moments of conflict or change, and knowing whether a shake flags "I am ending one activity and starting another" gives us a visible, countable marker of how an animal structures its own behavior. We can test this with an afternoon of careful observation, the same way primatologists test claims about scratching and yawning in monkeys.

The study we are testing

In 2024, Bryce, Nurkin, and Horowitz recorded 120 shakes from 96 dogs at a New York City dog run and daycare, and coded each dog's behavior for 10 seconds before and after the shake. Behavior differed across the shake in 107 of 120 events, and they concluded that shaking marks a transition between activities (Animals 14:3248, open access). They did not measure how often dog behavior changes across an ordinary 10-second window, so the study has no control sample. Today we collect that control and rerun their test properly.

Your jobs

  • Everyone, first 20 minutes: register the dogs already in the park, on the Dogs tab. Photograph each dog, ask the owner its name, age, and sex, and enter size and coat. The photos become the thumbnails every team uses to identify individuals. After that the gate team registers each new dog as it arrives.
  • Team A — Shake tab (all-occurrence sampling): scan the whole park and record every shake you see, with the behavior just before, the behavior in the 10 s after, tail position, and dogs within 1 m. If you did not see what came before, tap Not seen; an honest blank beats a guess.
  • Team B — Focal tab (focal sampling): pick a dog, code its behavior now, let the 10 s timer run, code its behavior at the beep, then pick a new dog and repeat. These windows measure the baseline rate of behavioral change, the control the original study lacks.
  • Team C — Gate tab: every dog that enters is one trial. Start a stopwatch at leash-off and record whether it shakes within 5 minutes; you can run several timers at once when dogs arrive together. You are also the registry for arriving dogs, so photograph and add each new one while its clock runs. Arrival is the one big transition we can see coming, so this station gives a guaranteed, standardized sample no matter how few shakes the rest of the park produces.

Ground rules

  • Record only shakes you watched yourself. If two of you log the same shake, good: matched double-sightings measure our inter-observer reliability.
  • Do not call, touch, or follow the dogs. We are measuring their behavior, not changing it.
  • Set your observer name (top right) before your first record.
  • No signal? Keep recording. Entries queue on your phone and upload when the connection returns.

Where these methods come from

All-occurrence sampling and focal sampling are the standard methods of primate field studies, defined by Jeanne Altmann in 1974 and used unchanged today. You are running the same protocol on an easier primate-watcher's subject: a fenced park full of habituated carnivores.

Individual differences

Dog personalities

Every dog in the park is doing the same things, in different proportions. Those proportions are what behavioral ecologists mean by personality.

Personality is a measurement, not a metaphor

When a biologist says an animal has a personality, the claim is specific and testable: individuals differ from each other consistently, and those differences hold up across time and situations. The informal version ("Rufus is the bold one") becomes science only when you measure the same individual repeatedly and show that it stays more bold than its neighbors.

Réale and colleagues (2007) organized the field around five axes that keep reappearing across species: shyness-boldness (response to risk), exploration-avoidance (response to novelty), activity, aggressiveness, and sociability. Our afternoon can touch sociability, activity, and a little of boldness.

Repeatability is the whole ballgame

The statistic that separates personality from noise is repeatability: the share of total behavioral variation that sits among individuals rather than within them. Watch one dog ten times and it will not behave identically; the question is whether its ten observations cluster tighter around its own average than around the park's average. A meta-analysis of 759 estimates across animals put average repeatability near 0.37, meaning roughly a third of behavioral variation is individual identity and two thirds is the moment.

Traits travel in packages

Sih and colleagues (2004) named a second pattern: behaviors correlate into behavioral syndromes. Bold animals tend also to be more exploratory, more active, and more aggressive. That matters because it means an animal cannot optimize each behavior separately; being bold enough to find food fast comes bundled with being bold enough to get eaten.

This is well documented in wild animals

  • Great tits in the Netherlands sort into fast and slow explorers. The difference is heritable, responds to artificial selection, and predicts survival differently in good and bad mast years.
  • Bighorn sheep ewes differ consistently in boldness and docility, measured over years of repeated handling, and those differences predict how they use risky terrain.
  • Three-spined sticklebacks and Trinidadian guppies show boldness syndromes that shift with how much predation their population has experienced.
  • Eastern chipmunks, dumpling squid, social spiders, and bluebirds all have documented, repeatable individual differences.

Why this matters for wildlife monitoring

Personality creates a sampling problem that reaches straight into camera trapping and live trapping. Bold, exploratory individuals approach novel objects sooner, so they walk in front of cameras and into traps at higher rates than shy ones. A sample of captured or photographed animals therefore over-represents one personality type, and estimates built from it, of density, of activity timing, of diet, inherit that bias. The effect has been measured directly: boldness predicts trappability in wild lizards, and different capture techniques select for different temperaments in the same population. Any detection model that assumes animals of a species are interchangeable is making an assumption this literature has shown to be false.

It also matters for conservation outcomes. Personality predicts which individuals disperse into new habitat, which ones persist in cities, and which ones end up in conflict with people. Translocation programs that move whichever animals are easiest to catch are, without meaning to, selecting on temperament.

What we can and cannot claim today

We will have a few observations per dog from one afternoon. That is enough to describe each dog's profile and to test whether the dogs differ more than sampling noise alone would produce. It is not enough to establish repeatability, because that needs the same dog measured on many separate occasions. Treat the profiles below as field sketches, and read the heterogeneity test as the real result.
